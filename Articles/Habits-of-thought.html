Habits of thought: How your brain hears music

Sometimes I sit alone at night, not because I am sad, depressed, or even because I am bored. I just got to the point where existence feels overwhelming in a way where loneliness brings me comfort. Yes, I may be sat by my girlfriend, but it’s 1:45 in the morning and she’s sleeping.

I tend to sit alone with music because it brings me a certain type of comfort, a comfort nothing else can genuinely bring me; that’s not to say there aren’t better comforts, sure I’d rather talk to someone and hold their hand, but sometimes music is what I need. It may be a habit, maybe it’s just what my brain wants, but who knows?

No one knows… We can’t know, we don’t exactly know how to figure out why music does this, do we? I mean yes, sound waves convert into electrical signals and that can cause dopamine to spike or activate your limbic system (the emotional part of the brain, such as the hippocampus and amygdala) but that doesn’t mean anything to a normal person.

Think of times before we had the EEG. Times before we could see into your brain and pinpoint what you feel with a machine. What did music mean then?

Why do we even listen to music if it really is just affecting our brainwaves?

Well, that one was given… We like it. Music feels good, I mean it feels good for a multitude of reasons. The rhythm can literally influence your brainwaves. I mean obviously music makes people emotional, whether it’s a sad song about losing one which you loved or a cheerful song about growth and redemption, music can move you.

But that’s not the only way music works in your head. The beat of music, through neural entrainment, can actually make your brain synchronize its electrical oscillations to the rhythm. The rhythm of the song, the pitch; it all connects to your brain in a way which makes emotions feel strong.

The rhythm of a song is a big part of what connects it to your brain. When you listen to Antonín Dvořák and you get goosebumps, that is your brain’s reward system responding to pleasure so strong it creates a physical reaction. Well maybe not all of you get goosebumps, you could also become calm or excited!

But if music works this way for everyone, why do we have such different perspectives and feelings towards it? I personally think that everyone likes different music specifically because of how our hippocampus processes the memories of the type of beat played in it.

In reality, music taste is rooted in psychological, social, and environmental factors. The place you grow up, the groups you hang out with, and the meaning of the song to someone will all change how you react to it. Some people listen to classical because they grew up playing piano, others listen to rap because they enjoy freestyling with their friends.

Every aspect of your life will have some effect on how you process music and it’s fascinating.

Personally I like most types of music. Whether it is 2Pac or Frédéric Chopin, it will feel good for me. I grew up listening to lots of rap with my parents, but I loved playing the keyboard whenever I could get my hands on one. These are the social and environmental factors I was talking about.

For psychological factors, you have to take into account good and bad experiences with it. I find that I dislike EDM and electric music because it is associated with bad parts of my life. And listening to pop is something I would do when happy so obviously I’d like it! Liking music isn’t the only aspect though.

Like I mentioned I can’t stand EDM, so what makes us actually dislike music as opposed to liking it? There are many causes; as I said earlier, bad experiences can cause you to dislike music, moreover, people can find it repetitive or lacking complexity.

There’s even a name for the utter lack of enjoyment of music, musical anhedonia. Musical anhedonia is a disconnect between the auditory and reward parts of your brain. This causes music to be perceived as completely neutral instead of pleasurable.

That must be a weird world. Perceiving something most people love as neutral could make you feel completely out of place. I wonder if the same could happen with art. Looking at a beautiful work of art, such as The Kiss by Gustav Klimt, someone might see… A generic blob of colors and patterns.

In a way, the younger me was like this. I disliked music and art because it didn’t make me feel anything, but that’s the beauty of it. Art is just colors formed to be beautiful. The only thing stopping anyone from noticing that is their own imagination and thoughts.

Nowadays, I’ll ogle at paintings while listening to music as if they’re the only things I’ve ever seen. It’s a weird obsession, but it shows just how much the brain can change through the years of one’s life.

The young brain doesn’t see the same way as ours

The young brain doesn’t feel emotion and see art the same way a post-adolescent brain does. They regulate emotions less well, because the parts of the brain that handle it, like the prefrontal cortex, are still developing into the mid-20s. They struggle with regulating emotions due to less strategic thinking and because they are more external with it, e.g. temper tantrums, yelling, hitting, etc.

This may be part of why they don’t process art the same. They often see it as boring, and they seem to engage with it differently. They’d even rather make art than look at a painting.

As for music, kids listen to it and they will love it, but their emotional and memory associations are still forming. They still get rhythmic entrainment and that reward, but they likely experience it differently. This doesn’t mean they can’t enjoy it like us, just that it’s a different experience.

So here I am again, awake at 1:45 in the morning with my girlfriend asleep next to me and music in my ears. I still can’t tell you exactly why it comforts me like it does. Maybe it’s the beat syncing up with my brain, maybe it’s the memories of growing up with rap and a keyboard, or maybe it’s both. But I’ve stopped needing a perfect answer, because the science only explains part of it. The rest is just what a song means to me, and that’s something no machine can measure.
